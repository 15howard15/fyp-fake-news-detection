Can AI-generated fake news train a fake-news detector?

Four research questions, four model families (Logistic Regression, SVM, CNN, BERT), evaluated on two unseen test corpora. Every number on this page is read directly from the project's results files at build time.

Training data: ISOT · Test data: LIAR and WELFake · Synthetic text: GPT-4o-mini

RQ1 — Can AI-written fake news replace real fake news for training?

Short answer

Partly, and only if the real-news side stays genuine. Replacing the fake class with synthetic articles keeps LR, CNN and BERT usable but costs accuracy, and breaks SVM completely. Replacing both classes produces a model that is confidently backwards.

Five training recipes, all evaluated on the same unseen LIAR test set. The first three vary only where the fake-news examples come from. The last two also make the real side synthetic, which is what exposes the shortcut described below.

The validity check: is it detecting fake news, or detecting AI writing?

If a model scores well on AI-generated fake news, it might have learned "this text was written by a machine" rather than "this claim is false" — a different and far less useful skill. The last two recipes test this by making the real class AI-written too.

AUC-ROC when both classes are AI-authored, vs. only the fake class
1.0 = perfect ranking · 0.5 = random guessing · below 0.5 = confidently backwards. Shown on both test sets so it can't be dismissed as one dataset's quirk.

F1 hides this; AUC-ROC exposes it. With both classes AI-written, AUC-ROC falls far below 0.50 for all four models on both test sets — the models did not become uncertain, they learned a consistent rule pointing the wrong way. The cause is in how the data was built: the "real" examples were AI-paraphrased throughout while the "fake" ones kept their original wording, so the models never saw genuine unedited writing labelled real.

RQ2 — Does adding synthetic fake news on top help, instead of replacing?

Short answer

Yes, but only in moderation and only for some models. A blend of roughly 25–50% synthetic holds or improves performance for LR, SVM and BERT. Past that SVM collapses and BERT falls away. CNN never benefits at any level.

The fake class is held at exactly 500 examples throughout — only the proportion that is synthetic changes. Holding the size fixed means any movement is attributable to the data's composition rather than to having more of it.

CNN is the exception worth explaining. Its errors are the same kind at every blend level — genuine real articles called fake, never the reverse — and the same stylistically distinctive articles (opinion-led rather than plain wire copy) keep reappearing. Its confidence on those mistakes falls from about 96% to 63% as synthetic data increases, so it is gradually losing its grip on a category it already found hard, not learning a new wrong rule the way SVM does.

RQ3 — Does the detector still generalize to fake news from a completely different source?

Short answer

It depends on the recipe, not on the model alone. Recipes built on real fake news transfer reliably. Full replacement transfers unevenly. And the second test corpus turns out to be much less independent than it appears, which changes how these numbers should be read.

Every recipe is evaluated against two unseen corpora: LIAR (short political statements) and WELFake (full-length articles). Using two rather than one is what makes it possible to tell a genuine finding from one dataset's quirk.

WELFake is not an independent corpus. Measured directly, of the WELFake fake articles used here are exact text matches for articles that also appear in ISOT, the training corpus — against 0.0% for LIAR. WELFake is a merged dataset that includes the same underlying collection ISOT derives from. Scores on WELFake are therefore closer to an in-domain test than a cross-domain one, and the gap between LIAR and WELFake is partly corpus overlap rather than genre.

Is the LIAR gap really about document length?

LIAR statements are far shorter than ISOT articles, so a natural explanation for weaker LIAR scores is length rather than domain. That is testable: hold the domain fixed, shorten the WELFake test set, and re-score the same models.

F1 as the test articles are progressively truncated
Domain held constant — only length changes. If short text were the problem, these lines would fall.

Length does not explain the LIAR gap — it works the other way. Shortening the test articles leaves performance flat or slightly higher. Truncating only the fake class, which reproduces LIAR's long-real / short-fake structure, raises F1 further still, because length itself becomes a usable clue. The LIAR gap is therefore a genuine domain effect, which makes the generalization challenge more real than a format artifact would have been.

RQ4 — Can style-diverse synthetic training resist stylistic and sentiment-based attacks?

Short answer

Yes, and it costs nothing in ordinary accuracy. Pairing every article with a tone-shifted twin under the same label drops the attack success rate to near zero. But generic synthetic data alone gives no such protection — and can make a model easier to fool.

200 held-out articles were rewritten to change only their tone: real articles made sensational, fake ones made calm and neutral, with every fact and label unchanged. The flip rate is how often a model that was originally correct changes its answer after the rewrite. Lower is better.

Flip rate after a tone-only rewrite, by training recipe
Lower is better — 0% means the tone change fooled the model on nothing it previously got right.

Read the two 0.0% cells under Real + Synthetic with care. SVM and CNN score 0.0% there not because they resist the attack but because that recipe already makes them predict one class for almost everything, leaving almost no correct predictions available to flip. A near-zero flip rate is only meaningful alongside a working baseline F1.

The style-robust recipe was built against one attack direction. Testing the reverse direction — real made calm, fake made sensational — on the same trained models, with no further training:

Not a trade-off. Every model performs as well or better under the style-robust recipe than under Real + Real on the untouched test set, so the robustness gain is real rather than a model becoming uniformly more cautious.

Validity checks

Checks that cut across all four questions — how stable the numbers are, whether the model comparison is fair, whether the generated data is any good, and whether any training text leaked into the test sets.

Seed stability — which findings are trustworthy?

LR and SVM are deterministic: the same data always gives the same answer. CNN and BERT start from random weights, so each reported number is one draw. All 18 reported model × recipe pairs were re-run at three seeds.

Cross-domain F1 across 3 seeds — mean, with the full observed range
The bar is the mean; the vertical line spans the lowest and highest of the three runs. Long lines mean the single-run number is unreliable.

BERT's core recipes are unstable; everything else is not. BERT swings widely on Real + Real, Real + Mixed and Real + Synthetic, so those single-run figures should be read as one draw from a wide range. The style-robust and diverse-sourced recipes are stable across seeds for both deep models, so the RQ4 and diverse-sourcing conclusions do not inherit that instability.

Is the model comparison fair?

By default the four models do not read the same amount of each article: TF-IDF has no length limit so LR and SVM read all of it, BERT stops at 512 tokens and CNN at 300 — and 64.5% of training articles are longer than 300 words. Retraining everything with all inputs truncated to the same 300 words removes that confound.

F1 with full text vs. all models capped at 300 words
Same recipe, same test set — the only change is that no model reads more of an article than another.

LR's advantage under full replacement is largely a length artifact. Given the same input as everyone else, LR loses the most of any model in any condition and drops from best to worst of the three working models on Real + Synthetic. The LR/SVM half of this is deterministic and exactly reproducible; the CNN/BERT half is a single run in BERT's least stable condition, so "LR's advantage disappears" carries much more weight than "BERT becomes best".

Is the generated text actually any good?

The only quality gate during generation was a length-ratio filter, which catches refusals and truncations but supports no claim about whether the text reads plausibly or whether the intended fact actually changed. Both were measured after the fact.

Plausibility rating carries a caveat. An LLM judge rates the synthetic articles above real fake news for reading like professional news writing — expected, since they are real articles with one fact altered while real fake news is stylistically distinctive low-quality writing. But the judge is the same model family that generated the text, so self-preference bias cannot be ruled out from this measurement alone.

Did any training text leak into the test sets?

ISOT contains duplicate articles, and because the split is done on rows rather than unique texts, a duplicated article can land on both sides. The resulting contamination is small — well under the 2% threshold the pipeline now enforces via evaluate.py leakage — and the main cross-domain test set is essentially unaffected.
