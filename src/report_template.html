5-fold cross-validation — and why the split has to be pair-aware

In-distribution AUC under an ordinary split vs. a pair-aware split
LR and SVM only — CNN and BERT keep their single-fit loop and their 3-seed runs, which measure the same variance far more cheaply. These are in-distribution scores: 80% of a recipe against the held-out 20% of that same recipe. They are not comparable to the cross-domain numbers above.

An ordinary k-fold split leaks here. The synthetic recipes are built as minimal pairs — an article and its one-fact-altered twin. An ordinary split puts one half of a pair in training and the other in validation, so the model memorises the article as real and calls its fake twin real too. That pushes AUC below 0.5 rather than merely lowering it: . Keeping each pair whole removes the effect, and recipes with no synthetic data are unmoved — which is what identifies the pairs as the cause.

Validity check: detecting fake news, or detecting AI writing?

AUC-ROC when both classes are AI-authored, vs. only the fake class
1.0 = perfect ranking · 0.5 = random guessing · below 0.5 = confidently backwards. Shown on both corpora so it can't be dismissed as one dataset's quirk.

Confidently backwards, not uncertain. AUC-ROC falls below 0.50 for all four models on both corpora. The models learned that AI-paraphrased text means “real”, so genuine unedited articles look fake to them.

What was the shortcut worth? Rebuilding the controls with the edit depth matched

The same controls, with both classes rewritten to the same depth
The real class was rewritten throughout while the fake class kept most of the source wording, so “close to the source” predicted “fake” with no reference to any fact. RQ2 — Does adding synthetic fake news on top of real data improve performance, rather than replacing it?

Performance as synthetic fake news replaces more of the fake class
X-axis: share of the 500 fake-news training examples that are AI-generated. Switch test corpus below — the shape holding on all three is what makes the trend trustworthy.
All metrics across the sweep

The trend is not a length artifact. Length carries no information inside this sweep — a word-counter scores on the training data at every blend level, because real fake news (378 words) and synthetic fake news (376) are the same length. The shape also reproduces on the in-domain corpus, where length alone scores 0.474. What moves across the sweep is the fake class's source, not its length.

The 100% endpoint is the exception. There the two corpora disagree sharply — LR reads 0.943 on LIAR but 0.638 in-domain, and CNN 0.065 against 0.424 — because at full replacement the models lean on the length gap LIAR hands them. That endpoint is the recipe rebuilt under length control in the framework tab.

CNN never benefits. The same error at every blend level — real articles called fake, never the reverse — and its confidence on those mistakes falls from 96% to 63%.

Cross-validated in-distribution, across the same sweep

5-fold CV AUC at each blend level, ordinary vs. pair-aware split
LR and SVM only. The gap between the two lines is the size of the minimal-pair effect, and it grows with the synthetic share — at 0% there are no pairs and the lines meet.

This is the sweep's own confound, and it is not the length one. Every synthetic article in these recipes is derived from a real article that is also present as a real-labelled row, so raising the synthetic share raises the number of near-duplicate pairs in lockstep. That inflates nothing on the cross-domain test sets — those articles aren't in them — but it makes ordinary cross-validation on these compositions unusable. The pair-aware line is the one to read.

Coverage note. The WELFake line is complete for LR, SVM and CNN, and has gaps at 25% and 75% for BERT only — the sweep script never saved BERT checkpoints at those two points, and scoring a model you didn't keep would mean retraining it. Those cells are left as gaps rather than interpolated, so a broken line means "not measured", never "measured and unremarkable".

Length control — can a word-counter beat the models?

How much of each test set is solvable from document length alone
A "classifier" that sees nothing but the word count. 0.5 means length tells you nothing. Anything near 1.0 means a high score on that test set is not by itself evidence of anything.

LIAR is almost entirely solvable by counting words. — its fake statements have a median of 16 words against 367 for the real articles. The WELFake sets sit near , so length is genuinely uninformative there.

Same recipe, length confound removed
Real + Synthetic against a twin built from synthetic fakes written at ~25, ~100 and ~400 words, with each real article cut to match its pair. Everything else — the pairing, the class balance, the manipulation types — is identical, so length is the only variable.
Both recipes on every test set

The length-controlled recipe is the better detector where it counts. On cleaned WELFake — the set where word-counting doesn't help — removing the confound raises AUC for three of the four models ( on average). On LIAR it changes scores in both directions: LR falls 0.943 → 0.690 and CNN rises 0.054 → 0.552. Both moves have the same cause. LR was riding the length gap and CNN was riding it backwards; neither was reading the facts.
