Final Year Project · Fake News Detection

Can AI-written fake news train a better fake-news detector?

Four detection methods were trained on real news and various mixes of real and AI-generated ("synthetic") fake news, then tested on fake news they never saw during training — including a completely different dataset, to check the results aren't just a fluke of one test set.

What each training recipe means

Every chart below compares the same three "recipes" — the only thing that changes between them is where the fake-news examples came from during training. Same amount of real news, same amount of fake news, every time.

Real + Real
Real news, and genuine fake news written by people. The baseline — no AI-generated text anywhere.
Real + Mixed
Real news, and fake news that's half genuine, half AI-generated.
Real + Synthetic
Real news, but every fake-news example was written entirely by an LLM — full replacement.
Objective 1 · Full replacement

Can AI-written fake news replace real fake news for training?

Real fake news is scarce and expensive to collect. If a detector trained purely on AI-generated fake news works just as well, that's a cheap substitute. Each bar below is how well a model, trained under one recipe, detects fake news it's never seen — from a completely different source (LIAR). The last two recipes go a step further and make the REAL side synthetic too (paraphrased by an AI, facts unchanged) — a validity check explained in the next section.

Detection accuracy (F1 score) by method and training recipe — full matrix
Higher is better. Tested on real news + LIAR fake news, never seen during training.
Real + Real Real + Mixed Real + Synthetic Synthetic real + Real fake Synthetic real + Synthetic fake
Full replacement breaks one method completely. SVM trained entirely on synthetic fake news scores F1 = 0.000 — it predicts "real" for every single test article. The synthetic fake articles are built by changing one fact in a real article and keeping everything else nearly identical, so SVM never learns a signal that generalizes beyond that narrow trick. The other three methods degrade too, just less catastrophically.

It's not just a low score — every single wrong prediction comes back at exactly 50% confidence, meaning the model isn't guessing wrong, it has zero ability to tell these apart at all. Compare a genuinely fake headline it misses entirely to a real one the baseline model still gets right:

Real + Synthetic · misclassified as real (50% confidence)
"NOT KIDDING: CNN Asks Widow Of Jewish Terror Victim If He Had It Coming? [VIDEO]. Islamic terrorist Syed Farook threatened his coworkers at work…" — obvious sensationalized clickbait, missed completely.
Real + Real · correctly identified as fake
Genuine fake-news examples in training let this model recognize the same kind of clickbait pattern — it fails on only 462 of 9,941 test articles, versus Real + Synthetic missing 5,657.
Beyond the proposal · Validity check

Is it detecting fake news, or just detecting AI writing?

A worry with the whole approach: when a model does well on AI-generated fake news, is it actually learning "this is false," or has it just learned "this was written by an AI"? Those are different things, and if it's the second one, the whole method is measuring the wrong signal. To test this, "synthetic real" news was also built — real articles paraphrased by an AI, facts unchanged — giving the last two recipes in the chart above, where both classes are machine-touched. Their F1 scores (0.50–0.61 for LR/SVM/BERT, 0.14 for CNN) look mediocre rather than catastrophic on their own — the chart below is what reveals the real problem.

AUC-ROC when both classes are AI-authored, vs. only the fake class
1.0 = perfect ranking, 0.5 = random guessing. Below 0.5 means the model's confidence is pointing the wrong way. Shown on both test sets so the pattern can't be dismissed as one dataset's quirk.
Only fake class is AI-written Both classes are AI-written
F1 alone hides the problem — AUC-ROC is what exposes it. The model isn't uncertain on "Synthetic real + Synthetic fake," it's confidently backwards: AUC-ROC sits well below 0.5 for all four methods, on both test sets (as low as 0.027 on LIAR, 0.053 on WELFake for CNN), and stable across 3 random seeds — not a fluke run. It even happens on same-source test data with no domain shift at all, which rules out "doesn't generalize" as the explanation.

Reading the actual misclassified articles (two independent methods, BERT and Logistic Regression, show the identical pattern) explains why:

Genuine real article · misclassified as fake (99.8% confidence)
"Targeting U.S. automaker signals possible China retaliation over Trump talk. WASHINGTON (Reuters) — China's plan to punish a U.S. automaker accused of price-fixing is a sign of how Beijing could retaliate…"
Genuine real article · misclassified as fake (94% confidence, different method)
"Trump in crisis after lewd remarks about women come to light. NEW YORK/WASHINGTON (Reuters) — Republican presidential candidate Donald Trump plunged into a deep crisis…"

The "real" training examples here were AI-paraphrased (different sentence structure throughout). The "fake" training examples were built by changing exactly one fact and keeping the original wording almost untouched. So the model never once saw genuine, unedited news copy labeled real during training — only rephrased text. At test time, real articles are, obviously, unedited — which makes them look more like what the model learned to call "fake" than what it learned to call "real." The model isn't confused; it learned a real, consistent, but backwards rule.

Objective 1 · Partial augmentation

Does adding synthetic fake news on top help, instead of replacing?

A softer question: keep every real fake-news example, and just add some AI-generated ones on top. This chart holds the total fake-news count fixed at 500 and only changes what fraction of it is synthetic, from 0% (all real) to 100% (all synthetic).

F1 score as synthetic fake news replaces more of the fake class
X-axis: % of the fake-news training examples that are AI-generated. Fake-class size held constant at 500.
LR SVM CNN BERT
A moderate blend (25–50% synthetic) is the sweet spot for LR, SVM and BERT — scores hold steady or improve. Past 50%, SVM collapses sharply and BERT drops off a cliff at 100%. CNN is the odd one out: it declines steadily the whole way, gaining nothing from synthetic data at any blend level.
Why CNN never finds a sweet spot: reading its actual wrong predictions at every blend level shows one consistent error, not a mix — genuine real articles predicted fake, never the other way round. The exact same specific real articles (colorful, opinion- or personality-driven pieces, not plain wire reports) keep reappearing as top mistakes as more synthetic data is added, and CNN's confidence on them actually drops the more synthetic data it sees (96% → 63% average confidence from 25% to 100% synthetic). That's a gradually eroding decision boundary on stylistically distinctive real writing, not CNN learning a new, confident shortcut the way SVM does — a different failure mode from every other method here.
Objective 1 · Diverse sources

Does it matter which real news the synthetic fake news is based on?

Every "Real + Synthetic" result so far used fake news generated from ONE source: real ISOT articles, lightly rewritten. That's a narrow well to draw from. This tests whether sourcing the synthetic generation from a second, independent dataset (LIAR) as well — instead of ISOT alone — fixes any of the specific weaknesses seen earlier, while keeping the total amount of fake-class training data exactly the same.

F1 score by training setup and test set
Same total fake-class size (500) throughout. Each panel names exactly what was trained and what it was tested on.
Tested on LIAR Tested on WELFake
Every model improves — and it fixes two things that were completely broken. SVM's F1 rises from 0.000 to 0.313, and more importantly its ranking ability (AUC-ROC) goes from pure chance (0.500) to near-perfect (0.976) — the earlier "predicts real for everything" collapse is gone. CNN's F1 rises from 0.711 to 0.897, and its AUC-ROC — which was inverted at 0.054 (confidently backwards) — recovers to 0.998. LR and BERT, which weren't broken to begin with, still improve (BERT reaches a near-perfect 1.000 F1 / AUC-ROC).
Diversifying where the synthetic fake news comes from doesn't just add more data — it removes the narrow, single-source "tell" that SVM and CNN were either failing to find or latching onto backwards. Same total training size, same total fake-class count, only the source mix changed.
Checked against a third dataset the training never touched (WELFake) — the fix mostly holds, with one exception. Since the LIAR-sourced synthetic data is built from LIAR's own real-labeled statements, it's fair to ask whether the improvement above is genuine, or just the model getting a preview of LIAR's writing style. WELFake was never touched by either synthetic-generation step, so it's a clean check. Ranking ability (AUC-ROC) improves for every method here too (SVM 0.50→0.66, CNN 0.45→0.86, LR 0.64→0.80, BERT 0.63→0.95) — real evidence this is about construction diversity, not a peek at the test domain. The exception: BERT's F1 on WELFake actually drops (0.71→0.30) despite its better ranking — precision stays near-perfect (0.999) but recall collapses to 0.18, meaning it becomes too conservative about calling anything fake. That looks like a default-threshold miscalibration specific to this composition/dataset pair, not a broken model, but it means the fix isn't a uniform win across every metric and every dataset.
Objective 2 · Cross-domain generalization

Does the detector still work on fake news from a totally different source?

Testing on LIAR (short political statements, ~11 words) is a harsh test for a model trained on full news articles (~250–380 words) — some of any failure could just be "never seen a document this short" rather than a real generalization problem. So every recipe used anywhere in this report was also tested against a second, independent dataset, WELFake, which — like ISOT — is made of full-length articles. Comparing the two tells apart a genuine content problem from a format problem, for all six recipes at once.

F1 score on two different unseen fake-news sources, all 6 recipes
Same trained models, two different test sets. LIAR is short statements; WELFake is full articles like the training data.
LIAR (short statements) WELFake (full articles)
Real + Real and Real + Mixed score noticeably higher on WELFake than on LIAR, for every method — confirming that part of the "poor generalization" seen on LIAR really is the short-format shift, not a content problem.
Real + Synthetic's SVM collapse happens on WELFake too (F1 still 0.000) — proving that specific failure is real and has nothing to do with LIAR's short format. Training only on AI-generated fake news genuinely doesn't transfer, regardless of what you test it against.
The other three recipes tell the same story, each in its own way. The authorship-shortcut inversion (Synthetic real + Synthetic fake) is confidently backwards on WELFake too, not just LIAR — part of why it's treated as a real finding rather than a fluke in the validity-check section. The diverse-sourcing fix (last panel) improves ranking ability for every model on WELFake as well as LIAR, which is what confirmed it wasn't just the model previewing LIAR's writing style — except for BERT, whose F1 on WELFake specifically regresses despite the better ranking, a threshold-calibration wrinkle covered in the "diverse sources" section.
Objective 4 · Style robustness

Can you fool the detector by just changing the tone?

A different kind of attack: take real held-out articles and have an LLM rewrite them — making real news sound more sensational, and fake news sound calm and neutral — without changing a single fact. The true label never changes. A model reading for content should barely notice; a model that's secretly using "sounds dramatic" as a shortcut for "fake" will get fooled. This chart shows BERT's flip rate: of the articles it got right originally, the fraction that became wrong after the tone-only rewrite.

BERT flip rate after a tone-only rewrite, by training recipe
Lower is better — 0% means the tone change fooled nobody.
Real + Mixed is the most easily fooled — 10.5% of originally-correct predictions flip after a tone-only rewrite. Generic synthetic fake news alone doesn't protect against style attacks; it can make things worse.
Training deliberately on both tones fixes it. A follow-up recipe ("style-robust") pairs each real article with a sensationalized twin still labeled real, and each fake article with a neutralized twin still labeled fake — so tone can no longer act as a shortcut for the label. Flip rate drops to 0.0%, with no loss in baseline accuracy: F1 on the original (non-attacked) test set is 0.868 (LR), 0.899 (SVM), 0.993 (CNN) and 0.991 (BERT) — flat-to-better than training on real fake news alone (0.857, 0.908, 0.957, 0.964 respectively), not a trade-off against it.
The fix generalizes to the opposite attack too, though not quite as completely. Everything above only tested one direction (real made dramatic, fake made calm). Testing the reverse (real made calm, fake made dramatic) on the same style-robust models: flip rate rises a little for every method — BERT 0.0% → 1.5%, CNN 0.5% → 2.0%, SVM 2.6% → 4.1%, LR 3.1% → 4.6% — but stays under 5% for all four, versus Real + Mixed's 10–18% on the original direction. The fix isn't narrowly overfit to the one attack it was built against; it generalizes, just not perfectly.

A concrete example — a real article, before and after the tone-only rewrite, that fooled the "Real + Mixed" model but not the style-robust one:

Before · correctly identified as real
"Treasury's Mnuchin: Difficult not to cut taxes for top 10 percent. WASHINGTON (Reuters) — U.S. Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin said on Friday that it was difficult for the Trump administration's tax plan not to cut taxes for the wealthiest earners…"
After · misclassified as fake
"Tax Cuts for the Rich: Mnuchin Sounds Alarm on Wealthy 10%! WASHINGTON (Reuters) — In a shocking revelation, U.S. Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin declared on Friday that the Trump administration's tax plan is almost certain to benefit the wealthiest earners!…"
