Can AI-generated fake news train a fake-news detector?

Four research questions, four model families (Logistic Regression, SVM, CNN, BERT). Every question is answered by training on ISOT and testing on unseen LIAR and WELFake data, so cross-domain generalization is the shared evaluation framework rather than a separate question.

Training: ISOT · Testing: LIAR and WELFake (both unseen) · Synthetic text: GPT-4o-mini

RQ1 — Can LLM-generated fake news replace real fake news for training?

Short answer

Partly, and only if the real-news side stays genuine. Replacing the fake class with synthetic articles keeps LR, CNN and BERT usable but costs accuracy, and breaks SVM entirely. Replacing both classes produces a model that is confidently backwards.

Five training recipes, each tested on unseen LIAR fake news. The first three vary only where the fake-news examples come from. The last two also make the real side synthetic, which is what exposes the shortcut described below.

Validity check: detecting fake news, or detecting AI writing?

If a model scores well on AI-generated fake news, it may have learned "this text was written by a machine" rather than "this claim is false" — a different and far less useful skill. The last two recipes test exactly that by making the real class AI-written too.

AUC-ROC when both classes are AI-authored, vs. only the fake class
1.0 = perfect ranking · 0.5 = random guessing · below 0.5 = confidently backwards. Shown on both corpora so it can't be dismissed as one dataset's quirk.

F1 hides this; AUC-ROC exposes it. With both classes AI-written, AUC-ROC falls far below 0.50 for all four models on both corpora — the models did not become uncertain, they learned a consistent rule pointing the wrong way. The cause is in how the data was built: the "real" examples were AI-paraphrased throughout while the "fake" ones kept their original wording, so no model ever saw genuine unedited writing labelled real.

RQ2 — Does adding synthetic fake news on top of real data improve performance, rather than replacing it?

Short answer

Yes, but only in moderation and only for some models. Roughly 25–50% synthetic holds or improves cross-domain performance for LR, SVM and BERT. Past that SVM collapses and BERT falls away. CNN never benefits at any level.

The fake class is held at exactly 500 examples throughout — only the proportion that is synthetic changes. Fixing the size means any movement reflects the data's composition rather than simply having more of it.

CNN is the exception worth explaining. Its errors are the same kind at every blend level — genuine real articles called fake, never the reverse — and the same stylistically distinctive articles (opinion-led rather than plain wire copy) keep reappearing. Its confidence on those mistakes falls from about 96% to 63% as synthetic data increases, so it is gradually losing its grip on a category it already found hard, not learning a new wrong rule the way SVM does.

RQ3 — Do transformer-based detectors offer more consistent robustness than deep-learning and traditional models?

Short answer

No — not once "consistent" is separated into its two meanings. BERT is the most stable model across training recipes and reaches the highest peaks, but it is by far the least stable across random seeds. The traditional models are perfectly reproducible yet swing the most when the training data changes. No family wins on both.

Consistency can mean two different things, and the families rank differently on each. One is robustness to what the model was trained on; the other is reproducibility of the same setup run again. Both are measured below.

Is the comparison even fair?

By default the four models do not read the same amount of each article: TF-IDF has no length limit so LR and SVM read all of it, BERT stops at 512 tokens and CNN at 300 — and 64.5% of training articles run longer than 300 words. Retraining everything with all inputs capped at the same 300 words removes that confound.

F1 with full text vs. all models capped at 300 words
Same recipes, same test set — the only change is that no model reads more of an article than another.

LR's advantage under full replacement is largely a length artifact. Given the same input as everyone else, LR loses more than any other model in any condition and drops from best to worst of the three working models on Real + Synthetic. The LR/SVM half of this is deterministic and exactly reproducible; the CNN/BERT half is a single run in BERT's least stable condition, so "LR's advantage disappears" carries far more weight than "BERT becomes best".

RQ4 — Can style-diverse synthetic training resist stylistic and sentiment-based attacks?

Short answer

Yes, and it costs nothing in ordinary accuracy. Pairing every article with a tone-shifted twin under the same label drops the attack success rate to near zero. But generic synthetic data alone gives no such protection — and can make a model easier to fool.

200 held-out articles were rewritten to change only their tone: real articles made sensational, fake ones made calm and neutral, with every fact and label unchanged. The flip rate is how often a model that was originally correct changes its answer after the rewrite. Lower is better.

Flip rate after a tone-only rewrite, by training recipe
Lower is better — 0% means the tone change fooled the model on nothing it previously got right.

Read the two 0.0% cells under Real + Synthetic with care. SVM and CNN score 0.0% there not because they resist the attack but because that recipe already makes them predict one class for almost everything, leaving almost no correct predictions available to flip. A near-zero flip rate only means something alongside a working baseline F1.

Does the fix survive an attack it wasn't trained for?

The style-robust recipe was built against one attack direction. Testing the reverse — real made calm, fake made sensational — on the same trained models, with no further training:

Does robustness cost ordinary accuracy? (no attack applied)

Not a trade-off. Every model performs as well or better under the style-robust recipe than under Real + Real on the untouched test set, so the robustness gain is real rather than a model becoming uniformly more cautious.

The cross-domain evaluation framework

Every question above is answered by training on ISOT and testing on data no model saw during training. That shared protocol — not a separate research question — is what binds RQ1 to RQ4 together. This tab covers how well it holds up.

WELFake is not an independent corpus. Measured directly, of the WELFake fake articles used here are exact text matches for articles that also appear in ISOT, the training corpus — against 0.0% for LIAR. WELFake is a merged dataset that includes the same underlying collection ISOT derives from. Scores on WELFake are therefore closer to an in-domain test than a cross-domain one, and the gap between the two corpora is partly corpus overlap rather than genre.

Is the LIAR gap really about document length?

LIAR statements are far shorter than ISOT articles, so a natural explanation for weaker LIAR scores is length rather than domain. That is testable: hold the domain fixed, shorten the WELFake test set, and re-score the same models.

F1 as the test articles are progressively truncated
Domain held constant — only length changes. If short text were the problem, these lines would fall.

Length does not explain the LIAR gap — it works the other way. Shortening the test articles leaves performance flat or slightly higher. Truncating only the fake class, which reproduces LIAR's long-real / short-fake structure, raises F1 further still, because length itself becomes a usable clue. The LIAR gap is therefore a genuine domain effect, which makes the generalization challenge more real than a format artifact would have been.

Is the generated text any good?

The only quality gate during generation was a length-ratio filter, which catches refusals and truncations but supports no claim about whether the text reads plausibly or whether the intended fact actually changed. Both were measured afterwards.

Diversity — is the generator repeating itself?

Fact-change verification. Where the full source article was saved the rate is , against where only a 1,000-character preview was kept — so the shortfall is a limit of the audit trail, not evidence that the edits failed.

The plausibility rating carries a caveat. An LLM judge rates the synthetic articles above real fake news for reading like professional news writing — expected, since they are real articles with one fact altered while real fake news is stylistically distinctive low-quality writing. But the judge is the same model family that generated the text, so self-preference bias cannot be ruled out from this measurement alone.

Did any training text leak into the test sets?

ISOT contains duplicate articles, and because the split is done on rows rather than unique texts, a duplicated article can land on both sides. The resulting contamination is small — well under the 2% threshold the pipeline now enforces via evaluate.py leakage — and the main cross-domain test set is essentially unaffected.
